Mirza International: manufacturing what it no longer gets to sell

Three years after demerging its most profitable asset — the Redtape consumer brand — India's oldest listed leather exporter is posting back-to-back quarterly losses, absorbing a credit downgrade and an unresolved income-tax search, losing the CFO who steered it for three decades, and now studying whether to break itself up again.

Context

A sector growing 25% a year — and a company shrinking through it

India's leather and footwear export industry is having a genuinely good run. According to the Council for Leather Exports, the sector's exports jumped roughly 25% to $5.7 billion in FY25, and are on track to cross $6.5 billion in FY26 — with footwear alone accounting for close to half that mix. Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal has publicly urged the industry to target $15 billion in exports within five to six years. Backing that ambition is real government money: the Indian Footwear and Leather Development Programme (IFLDP) carries a ₹1,700 Crore outlay through March 2026, split across named sub-schemes — ₹500 Cr for Integrated Development of the Leather Sector, ₹500 Cr for Sustainable Technology and Environmental Promotion, ₹300 Cr for Mega Leather Footwear and Accessories Cluster Development, and ₹200 Cr for institutional facilities.

Mirza International is, on paper, exactly the kind of company that scheme was built for — a Kanpur/Unnao-origin, vertically integrated tannery-to-shoe manufacturer with over four decades of export relationships into the UK, US and Europe. Which is what makes its own numbers so jarring: standalone revenue has fallen from a peak of ₹1,386 Cr (FY22) to ₹517 Cr (FY26), and CRISIL's own rating rationale for the company cites "weak export demand" as a live risk in a year the broader export data was growing 25%. Some of that gap is structural and self-inflicted, not demand-driven: in 2023 the company demerged its own consumer-facing brand, Redtape — which accounted for roughly 55% of pre-demerger sales and about half of profit — into a separately listed company, keeping for itself the lower-margin tannery and contract-manufacturing engine underneath it. Now is a relevant moment to look closely because that engine, three years on, is showing consecutive quarterly operating losses, a fresh credit downgrade, an unresolved income-tax search, and a board-approved study (April 2026) to break the remaining company into three more pieces.

India's leather/footwear exports are growing ~25% a year toward a $15B government-backed ambition; Mirza International removed itself from that story's most valuable layer in 2023 and is now shrinking inside what's left.
2
The business model

Hide to shoe, sold under someone else's name

In plain terms, Mirza International buys raw hides, tans them into finished leather at its own tanneries in Kanpur and Unnao (Uttar Pradesh), cuts and stitches that leather into finished shoes at its own factories — including the Euro Footwear unit in Unnao — and ships the finished product to international retailers and brands under private-label and white-label arrangements. It also runs a smaller trading business in garments and accessories. What it does not do, since August 2023, is sell shoes under its own consumer brand: that business, Redtape, was demerged into a separately listed company, Redtape Limited, on a 1:1 share basis.

The loop that remains is a manufacturing one, not a brand one: hide and tanning-chemical input costs → in-house tanned leather (a genuine vertical-integration advantage few peers can replicate given the capital and environmental-compliance investment already sunk into the tanneries) → finished footwear manufactured to a buyer's specification → sold to long-standing private-label clients such as the Hush Puppies division of British Shoe Corporation, the Oliver Group (UK), and Clarks' Saxone brand (Australia). The stickiness in this model comes from decades-old buyer relationships and tannery capacity, not from consumer loyalty — which means Mirza now competes on price and reliability as a supplier, in a segment its own credit rating agency describes as facing "weak export demand," rather than on brand economics. International distribution has historically run through promoter-linked entities — Mirza UK Limited and, until a 2026 amalgamation, a Dubai (Jebel Ali Free Zone)-based entity called RTS Fashion Limited — a structure that has now been folded back into the listed parent, the second such consolidation in four years.

Mirza kept the capital-intensive tannery-and-factory engine and gave away the higher-margin consumer brand — leaving a supplier business that competes on price, not on brand pull.

Competitive structure

Porter's Five Forces

ForceRatingRationale
Rivalry intensityMediumIn branded retail, Mirza no longer competes directly — Bata India (₹15,693 Cr market cap, PE ~67x), Relaxo Footwears (₹8,637 Cr, PE ~45x) and Liberty Shoes (₹407 Cr, PE ~36x) now occupy that space, alongside Mirza's own former brand, Redtape. Buyer powerHighPrivate-label buyers (British Shoe Corporation's Hush Puppies, Oliver Group UK, Clarks' Saxone) are large global retail groups that can and do shift sourcing between manufacturers; Mirza's historical reliance on promoter-linked distribution entities (Mirza UK, the former RTS Fashion Dubai entity) for market access adds an additional layer of buyer concentration risk that is not fully transparent from public disclosures.
Supplier powerMediumRaw hide and tanning-chemical costs are globally traded commodities exposed to price volatility; in-house tanning (the Tannery Division) reduces dependence on external finished-leather suppliers but does not insulate the company from hide-price swings or chromium/tanning-agent input costs.
Threat of new entrantsLowA functioning tannery requires large upfront capital plus mandatory Common Effluent Treatment Plant compliance — regulators shut roughly 300 Kanpur-Unnao tanneries in 2018-19 alone over untreated discharge into the Ganga. That environmental and capital barrier genuinely limits new tannery licensing in the region, even as it burdens existing operators like Mirza with ongoing compliance capex.
Threat of substitutesHighSynthetic and "vegan" leather alternatives are gaining share globally on cost and sustainability positioning, and private-label buyers can redirect sourcing to non-leather-footwear factories with far lower capital and compliance barriers than a tannery-based supplier.

Synthesis: buyer power and threat of substitutes are the two highest-pressure forces — Mirza's genuine moat (tannery capital and environmental-compliance barriers to new entrants) does not protect it if its largest private-label buyers can simply redirect sourcing to synthetic-material or lower-cost suppliers instead.

Mirza's real barrier to entry (tannery capex + compliance) sits on the supply side; the forces actually squeezing its margins — buyer power and substitutes — sit on the demand side, where that barrier offers no protection.

The vertical stories

Three divisions, soon to be studied as three companies

Tannery DivisionUpstream · compliance-heavy

Converts raw hides, wet blue and crust into finished leather at the company's Kanpur/Unnao facilities — the vertical-integration anchor of the whole business, and the single most capital- and compliance-intensive piece of it. The government's IFLDP scheme earmarks ₹500 Cr specifically for "Sustainable Technology and Environmental Promotion" — a sub-scheme this exact division is positioned to draw on, given the Common Effluent Treatment Plant obligations the wider Kanpur-Unnao tannery cluster has faced since regulators shut roughly 300 non-compliant tanneries in 2018-19.

This is also the division most exposed if the board's April 2026 segregation study proceeds: whichever entity inherits the Tannery Division likely inherits its environmental-compliance liabilities and capex obligations along with its vertical-integration value.

Footwear Manufacturing & Private Label ExportCore revenue engine

Designs, manufactures and exports finished leather shoes to private-label and white-label clients across the UK, US and Europe — including long-standing relationships with the Hush Puppies division of British Shoe Corporation, the Oliver Group (UK) and Clarks' Saxone (Australia). This is the segment riding the sector's 25% export growth in principle, and the one CRISIL specifically flagged for "weak export demand" and narrowing operating margins (6.4% in FY25, down from 7.7%) in practice.

The gap between the sector's own growth numbers and this division's margin compression is the clearest sign that Mirza's problem is company-specific execution and positioning, not a demand-side sector headwind.

Garments / Accessories TradingSmallest, least differentiated

A trading operation in garments and accessories with no tannery-style vertical-integration advantage behind it — the smallest and lowest-conviction of the three divisions the board is now studying for potential segregation. Its inclusion in the same restructuring conversation as the Tannery and Footwear divisions raises the obvious question of what a standalone version of this business would actually be worth on its own.

Overseas distribution structureNot a segment — a recurring pattern

Marketing and distribution into the UK, US and Europe has historically run through promoter-linked entities — Mirza UK Limited, and until May 2026, a Jebel Ali Free Zone (Dubai)-based company called RTS Fashion Limited, which NCLT approved amalgamating into the listed parent (appointed date 1 April 2025, effective 1 May 2026) alongside settlement of a ₹7.27 Cr outstanding tax demand. This is the second time in four years a similarly-named "RTS" entity has been folded into or carved out of Mirza International — RTS Fashions Private Limited was amalgamated into the company as part of the very same 2023 scheme that demerged Redtape out. The pattern itself is a signal: this management team reaches for corporate restructuring — demergers, amalgamations, now a segregation study — as a recurring tool, not a one-off event.

The board is studying a three-way split of Tannery, Footwear and Garments — the same restructuring playbook that produced the 2023 Redtape demerger, applied again to what's left behind.
4
The strategic insight

A "return to profitability" built on one unexplained quarter

The single most important thing to understand about Mirza International today is that its FY26 "return to profitability" headline is real only on a full-year, standalone, exceptional-item-inclusive basis — and it obscures an operating business that got measurably worse every quarter in the second half of the year. Standalone PAT of ₹2.13 Cr for FY26 (versus a ₹3.99 Cr loss in FY25) includes an exceptional/other-income gain of roughly ₹18.6 Cr booked substantially in the June 2025 quarter — a gain whose precise composition has not been clearly broken out in the public disclosures reviewed for this brief. Strip that one item out, and the underlying FY26 standalone result is a loss of roughly ₹16.5 Cr. The quarterly cadence tells the real story: a ₹17.67 Cr net profit in Q1 FY26 (flattered by that other-income spike), narrowing to ₹3.99 Cr in Q2, then losses of ₹7.83 Cr in Q3 and ₹11.69 Cr (standalone) / ₹13.22 Cr (consolidated) in Q4 — the widest quarterly loss of the year, on the lowest quarterly revenue in recent periods (₹102.56 Cr, down 13.24% sequentially).

Layer that quality-of-earnings problem onto three other facts landing in the same twelve months: a CRISIL downgrade of the company's ₹215 Cr bank facilities to BBB+/Negative from A-/Negative, citing subdued operating income and two straight years of margin decline; an Income Tax Department search-and-seizure operation under Section 132 across more than 45 locations in Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Uttarakhand and Kolkata since September 2025, with no written outcome disclosed as of the FY26 results; and a board-approved study (25 April 2026) to segregate the remaining business into three separate legal entities — the same demerger playbook used on Redtape in 2023, now applied to what's left. Revenue has fallen from ₹1,386 Cr (FY22) to ₹517 Cr (FY26) and ROCE has collapsed from a historical 20-25% band to under 1% — a decline that is only partly explained by the 2023 demerger itself, since the manufacturing core that remained has kept shrinking and losing margin every year since.

Put simply: this is not yet a turnaround story. It is a company still in the middle of disassembling itself, where each restructuring event changes what a shareholder actually owns, and where the one number that looked like good news this year needs an asterisk next to it.

FY26's full-year profit exists only because of one large, unclearly-explained gain in a single quarter — the underlying business posted operating losses in both of the year's final two quarters.

Risks — eyes open

Five specific ways this goes wrong

Unresolved income-tax search, nearly a year in

The September 2025 Section 132 search across 45+ locations (Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Uttarakhand, Kolkata) has produced no written communication of findings as of the FY26 results. Management's statement that it expects "no material adverse impact" is an assertion, not a resolved fact — auditors flagged it as a Key Audit Matter, meaning the eventual quantum, if any, is currently unquantifiable.

Deepening quarterly operating losses

Q3 and Q4 FY26 both posted operating losses on a sequentially shrinking revenue base (Q4 revenue down 13.24% QoQ to ₹102.56 Cr, the lowest quarterly figure in recent periods). The full-year "profit" narrative masks an H2 trend that, if it continues into FY27, would erase the year's technical gain entirely.

Live negative credit outlook

CRISIL's downgrade to BBB+/Negative on ₹215 Cr of bank facilities explicitly cites subdued operating income, margin decline over two fiscal years, weak export demand and high working-capital intensity — a negative outlook that leaves room for a further downgrade if FY27 doesn't stabilise.

Serial corporate restructuring with undetermined terms

Three reorganisations in four years — the 2023 Redtape demerger and RTS Fashions amalgamation, the 2026 RTS Fashion (Dubai) amalgamation, and the April 2026 board-approved study to segregate Tannery, Footwear and Garments into separate entities — mean shareholders are repeatedly being asked to hold a company whose corporate perimeter keeps changing, with the newest split's structure, ratios and tax treatment entirely undetermined.

Veteran CFO exit during the weakest results in years

CFO V.T. Cherian's resignation (effective end-May 2026) lands in the same results cycle as the year's widest quarterly loss and the CRISIL downgrade. His successor is an internal promotee (General Manager since February 2024) rather than an external hire — a continuity choice that cuts both ways on a balance sheet now under active regulatory and rating-agency scrutiny.

No single risk here is the whole story — it's that a tax search, a credit downgrade, a leadership exit and a corporate restructuring are all unresolved at the same time.

Watch signals

Five things to check every quarter

Q1 FY27 operating result

The first quarter clean of the FY26 exceptional gain and the 2023 demerger's year-on-year comparison noise — does the Q3/Q4 FY26 operating-loss pattern continue, or does it stabilise?

Any written outcome from the September 2025 IT search

A formal demand notice or assessment order would finally quantify what is currently an unquantified contingent risk sitting alongside the ₹197 Cr of contingent liabilities already on the balance sheet.

Concrete terms of the segregation study

Watch for a record date, entity structure, or NCLT filing following the 25 April 2026 board approval — and specifically whether the Tannery Division's environmental-compliance liabilities travel with it or stay with the parent.

Next CRISIL / ICRA rating action

Whether the Negative outlook on the BBB+ rating is maintained, resolved, or cut further at the next review — the clearest external signal of whether FY27 is actually stabilising.

Disclosure of the ₹18.6 Cr exceptional item's composition

If the FY26 annual report notes finally clarify what this one-off gain actually was, that materially changes how much of the "return to profitability" headline an investor should trust.

The single most informative data point next quarter isn't revenue growth — it's whether the operating loss trend from H2 FY26 persists once the one-off gain is out of the base.
The Verdict

Mirza International sits inside a genuinely strong sector tailwind — Indian leather and footwear exports growing ~25% annually toward a government-backed $15 billion ambition — but it removed itself from that tailwind's most valuable layer when it demerged the Redtape brand in 2023, keeping the lower-margin manufacturing engine underneath. The quality signal in the financials is weak: ROCE has collapsed from a 20-25% historical band to under 1%, working capital has re-widened, and FY26's headline "return to profitability" depends entirely on one large, unclearly-explained one-off gain, while the underlying business posted operating losses in the year's final two quarters. Genuine positives exist — an almost debt-free balance sheet, no promoter share pledge, and a promoter stake that actually rose (71.27% to 72.99%) through the year's weakest results — but they sit alongside a live CRISIL downgrade, an unresolved income-tax search across 45+ locations, a departing 30-year CFO, and a board now studying a third corporate restructuring in four years. Valuation offers little comfort: Mirza trades near the same PE band as consistently profitable peers like Liberty Shoes, despite negative ROE and an earnings quality problem. This reads as a late-chapter distress story still being written — not yet a turnaround, and not yet resolved, with the next two quarters likely to determine which way it breaks.
