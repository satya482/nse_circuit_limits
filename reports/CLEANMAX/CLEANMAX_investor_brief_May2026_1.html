The big picture

The AI electricity crisis is arriving — and CleanMax is the landlord

India's commercial and industrial sector consumes roughly 40–45% of all electricity in the country — and it is the fastest-growing buyer of renewable energy. Grid tariffs for industrial consumers run ₹6–8 per unit across states. Renewable energy via open access or captive structures costs 20–40% less. The math writes itself: any CFO who can save 30% on electricity bills while ticking the ESG box will sign a long-term PPA.

Now add the AI layer. Global data centre electricity demand grew 17% in 2025 and is on course to nearly double to 945 terawatt-hours by 2030 (IEA). India's data centre capacity is projected to surge from 1.4 GW in 2024 to 9 GW by 2030 — a 15% annual growth rate, four times faster than total electricity consumption growth. Every major hyperscaler — Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Apple — has committed to 100% renewable energy. They need someone to build, own, and deliver that renewable energy reliably. That is CleanMax's business.

India's 500 GW renewable energy target by 2030 is the structural policy backdrop. The C&I segment alone requires close to 80 GW of new renewable capacity to meet even 20% of its energy needs through renewables, per ICRA estimates. CleanMax, with 3.1 GW operational and 5.7 GW contracted, has barely scratched the surface of that opportunity.

Sector signal: India's data centre capacity is forecast to grow 6.4× by 2030. Global hyperscaler capex exceeded USD 400 billion in 2025 and is projected to jump another 75% in 2026 (IEA). CleanMax's Data & AI portfolio grew 10× in two years — from 254 MW in FY24 to 2,380 MW in FY26.

The business

The OPEX model: CleanMax takes the risk so corporates don't have to

CleanMax pioneered the "energy sale" OPEX model for C&I solar in India in 2012. The concept is simple but powerful: CleanMax builds, owns, and operates the renewable energy asset. The corporate customer signs a long-term Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) and pays only for the electricity consumed — zero upfront investment, no asset on their balance sheet, no performance risk. For a finance director, it reads like paying a utility bill, except the power is 20–40% cheaper and comes with zero carbon.

There are three delivery structures. Under Third-Party Open Access, CleanMax owns everything and sells power at PPA tariff. Under the Group Captive model, the corporate invests a minimum 26% equity in the project SPV, which qualifies it for exemption from cross-subsidy surcharges — delivering even lower effective tariffs and more regulatory stability. Under the CAPEX model, the customer owns the asset and CleanMax does engineering, procurement, and construction. The OPEX and Group Captive routes together account for the bulk of CleanMax's revenues and margins.

The customer quality is remarkable: approximately 85% of customers are AAA-rated or subsidiaries of multinational companies. This is not a portfolio of small businesses — it is Amazon, Apple, Adobe, Equinix, Tata Motors, Volvo, Shell. Counterparty credit risk is lower than most DISCOM-exposed utility IPPs. And 78% of contracted capacity in FY25 came from repeat customers — businesses that returned to expand their existing renewable commitment.

The non-obvious insight

Don't read the P/E ratio — read the contracted capacity backlog

The most common mistake investors make with CleanMax is looking at the P/E ratio — approximately 176× on FY26 trailing PAT of ₹85.6 Cr — and concluding the stock is absurd. That conclusion misunderstands how infrastructure companies work. PAT is depressed by depreciation and interest charges on a large and rapidly growing asset base. These costs are fixed and front-loaded; the revenues they generate extend for 20–25 years.

The right frame is EV/EBITDA. With a market cap of ~₹15,000 Cr and estimated net debt of ~₹9,500 Cr (after IPO debt repayment of ₹1,122 Cr from ~₹10,700 Cr), the enterprise value is roughly ₹24,500 Cr. FY26 EBITDA was ₹1,295 Cr. That gives EV/EBITDA of ~19× — not cheap, but not absurd for a platform growing operational capacity at 80% per year with a 5.7 GW contracted backlog.

Here is the arithmetic that matters: CleanMax's 3.1 GW operational portfolio generates ~₹1,295 Cr EBITDA. The 2.6 GW under construction will, when commissioned over the next 24 months, add roughly ₹1,000+ Cr of incremental EBITDA — without winning a single new customer. The contracted backlog is effectively locked-in future EBITDA. This is why institutional investors — 29.8% FII ownership post-listing, including large funds — are comfortable holding at current prices. They are not buying today's PAT; they are buying the NPV of 25-year PPA cash flows from a 5.7 GW contracted pipeline.

The one thing to remember: CleanMax's P/E is meaningless. The right question is: what is 5.7 GW of contracted, annuity-like renewable energy revenue from AAA-rated corporates worth? The 2.6 GW backlog represents ~₹1,000+ Cr of EBITDA not yet on the income statement.

Eyes open

Five real risks — this is a highly leveraged infrastructure bet

⚠
Leverage — Net Debt/EBITDA at 4.75× with interest coverage under pressure
CleanMax carried total borrowings exceeding ₹10,000 Cr at IPO (September 2025), with net debt ~₹7,645 Cr. After IPO proceeds of ~₹1,122 Cr used for debt repayment, net debt/EBITDA is still ~4.75× at FY26 end. Interest costs are the primary reason PAT is only ₹85.6 Cr on ₹1,295 Cr EBITDA. Screener flags "low interest coverage ratio." Any rise in interest rates adds directly to interest costs without a corresponding revenue offset in the short term.
Red flag: Net Debt/EBITDA rising above 5× or interest coverage dropping below 1.5×
⚠
Data and AI concentration — 42% of contracted portfolio in a single new segment
The data centre and AI segment grew from 254 MW (FY24) to 2,380 MW (FY26) — a tenfold increase in two years. That is 42% of CleanMax's contracted portfolio in a segment that did not meaningfully exist in the company's portfolio three years ago. If data centre build-out slows (due to AI winter, hyperscaler capex pullback, or regulatory constraints on power-hungry data centres), a large chunk of CleanMax's projected future EBITDA is at risk of delay or cancellation before commissioning.
Red flag: Data & AI as a share of new signings dropping below 25% for two consecutive quarters; any hyperscaler PPA cancellation
⚠
Execution and grid connectivity — 2.6 GW under construction is ambitious
FY27 guidance of commissioning over 1.5 GW implies executing more in one year than CleanMax's total operational base just two years ago. India's renewable sector suffers from transmission bottlenecks, land acquisition delays, and regulatory uncertainty. Projects during FY26 were executed at 97% of budgeted costs — a strong record — but maintaining this at 1.5+ GW per year is a different challenge. Grid connectivity for the 525 MW CTU Bikaner project required navigating central transmission utility approvals that many developers struggle with.
Red flag: Commissioning guidance downgrade below 1.2 GW for FY27
⚠
PAT quality — "other income" contributed 58.6% of PBT in Q4 FY26
In Q4 FY26, CleanMax's operating margin compressed sharply from 62.3% (Q3 FY26) to 48.0%. Simultaneously, "other income" contributed 58.6% of profit before tax — including FX gains and investment income. The EBITDA trajectory is structurally sound, but the PAT journey from ₹19.4 Cr to ₹85.6 Cr was partly aided by non-operating items. Watch for operating EBITDA conversion to PAT to improve organically as debt reduces, rather than relying on other income.
Red flag: Other income contribution to PBT exceeding 50% for more than two quarters
⚠
Policy and regulatory risk — open access rules vary by state
India's renewable open access framework is set at the central level, but cross-subsidy surcharges and additional surcharges are state-level decisions. Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh have historically imposed high surcharges that make third-party open access uneconomical, forcing CleanMax to use the group captive route instead. Any state changing its surcharge structure can render existing or planned projects less competitive. Green Open Access rules notified in 2022 have not been uniformly implemented by all State Electricity Regulatory Commissions.
Red flag: Key states (Gujarat, Karnataka, Rajasthan) imposing materially higher cross-subsidy surcharges

Verdict · May 2026

CleanMax is a structurally differentiated bet on two of the most powerful trends in India's economy: the corporate decarbonisation mandate (ESG pressure, net-zero commitments, cheaper green power vs grid) and the AI data centre electricity boom (India's data centre capacity expected to grow 6.4× by 2030, with hyperscalers committing to 100% renewable supply). The quality signal in the business is the customer base — 85% AAA-rated or MNC, 78% repeat customers, 25-year PPA contracts — which creates annuity revenues that put utilities to shame on counterparty quality. The financial narrative requires the right lens: ignore P/E (~176×), use EV/EBITDA (~19×) and focus on the backlog: 2.6 GW under construction represents ~₹1,000 Cr of EBITDA not yet on the income statement. That backlog commissions over 24 months, mechanically growing EBITDA without needing new customers. The key re-rating catalyst is sustained FY27 commissioning above 1.5 GW — each quarterly commissioning announcement is a cash flow milestone the market will reward. The risks are real: 4.75× net debt/EBITDA is elevated, Data & AI at 42% of contracted portfolio is concentrated, and PAT quality needs to improve as other income dependency normalises. We are in the early-middle chapters — the operating model is proven over 14 years and 555 customers, but CleanMax only listed in February 2026 and has not yet demonstrated its execution capabilities at 1.5+ GW annual commissioning scale. Investors with a 3–5 year horizon and tolerance for leverage can hold confidently. Those expecting near-term PAT momentum should wait for the first full year of post-IPO commissioning data in FY28 before sizing up.
