Chapter One

Context — The Big Picture First

India is in the middle of a steel capacity supercycle that is only about a third complete. From JSW Steel and ArcelorMittal Nippon Steel to Tata Steel and SAIL, every major producer is investing heavily in both raw capacity and — crucially — the quality of what they produce. The shift is structural: India consumed 136 million tonnes of steel in FY26 and the Ministry of Steel targets 300 MT capacity by 2030. The India Hot Rolled and Cold Rolled Steel market is growing at 8.5% CAGR through 2030, driven by automotive, infrastructure, and the rapidly growing EV supply chain.

But the real acceleration is happening in the segment that matters most for John Cockerill India: specialty and coated steel. India's PLI Scheme for Specialty Steel has attracted commitments of ₹43,800 Cr (plus a further ₹11,887 Cr in PLI 1.2), targeting the addition of 8.7 million tonnes of high-grade capacity by FY31. This includes cold-rolled grain-oriented (CRGO) electrical steel for power transformers, galvanized and colour-coated products for automotive and construction, and advanced high-strength steels. These are products that cannot be made on a blast furnace alone — they require precisely engineered processing lines. That is the machine John Cockerill India builds.

The JSW-JFE Steel joint venture announced in August 2025 — a ₹5,845 Cr investment to expand CRGO capacity to 350,000 TPA by FY28 — is a direct order-pipeline catalyst for JCIL, which has already won the annealing and coating line contract for this project. PM Gati Shakti, the National Infrastructure Pipeline, and the National Green Hydrogen Mission layer further momentum on top. For JCIL, the timing is as good as it has ever been.

India's PLI Specialty Steel commitments total ₹43,800 Cr; JCIL builds the equipment that earns the PLI.

Chapter Two

The Business Model + Company Journey

How John Cockerill India Makes Money

JCIL designs, manufactures (at its Taloja and Hedavali facilities in Raigad, Maharashtra), and commissions the processing lines that transform raw steel coils into finished products — galvanized sheet for cars and appliances, electrical-grade steel for EV motors and transformers, colour-coated sheet for roofing, and high-strength steel for infrastructure. The company does not make steel; it makes the machine that makes the steel valuable.

The revenue flywheel works like this: a first project win with a steelmaker (say, a continuous galvanizing line) creates an installed base. The company then becomes the preferred supplier for the customer's next line, its expansion, its revamp — and for spares and maintenance throughout the 20–25 year life of the equipment. Today, services and spares represent 20–25% of JCIL's order book, up sharply from prior years, and this segment carries structurally higher margins than project work.

What makes customers sticky? Three things: (1) technology lock-in — JCIL's proprietary JVD coating technology and its exclusive Indian licence for the process means customers cannot simply switch to a competitor mid-product-lifecycle; (2) certification cycles — qualifying a new equipment supplier for a critical steel process can take 12–18 months; (3) site knowledge — JCIL has commissioned lines for JSW, Tata Steel, AMNS Hazira, Godawari Power, and Jindal India, and institutional memory from commissioning is irreplaceable.

Chapter Three

Competitive Dynamics — Porter's Five Forces

Force 1

Competitive Rivalry

🟡 Moderate Threat

Global peers are SMS Group, Danieli, and Primetals Technologies — all European heavyweights with global scale. In India, JCIL holds an established installed base with JSW, Tata Steel, AMNS, Godawari, and Jindal. Management explicitly claims a win-rate "superior to competitors." Chinese equipment makers are emerging at lower price points but lack the technology certification for specialty lines (CRNO, JVD).

Investor implication: Pricing power is intact for high-complexity lines. Margin pressure is most likely at the lower end (simple pickling, standard CGL). High-end technology lines — which are the growth vector — face limited Indian competition.

Force 2

Threat of New Entrants

🟢 Low Threat

Entering the high-end steel processing line market requires 12–18 months of customer qualification, proprietary technology IP (JCIL holds exclusive Indian rights to JVD, co-developed with ArcelorMittal), and physical engineering capability that takes a decade to build. JCIL's Taloja facility (machining, assembly) and Hedavali (heavy fabrication) represent a barrier that cannot be replicated quickly. The roll coating facility (HP-HVAF technology, first in India) inaugurated in 2026 deepens the moat further.

Investor implication: Structural moat. New domestic entrants are 5–7 years away from competing on complex lines. Chinese entrants face import perception risk given the "Make in India" procurement preference of JSW and Tata.

Force 3

Bargaining Power of Buyers

🟡 Moderate Threat

JSW Steel is clearly the anchor customer — the most recent ₹1,300 Cr CRNO order (JSW Vijayanagar Metallics), the ₹300 Cr CGL#3 (JSW Steel Coated Products), and a ₹1,750 Cr earlier JSW order suggest JSW accounts for a very large share of the CY2025–26 order book. AMNS (ArcelorMittal), Tata Steel, Godawari, and Jindal India diversify the base. Contract structures appear long-term project-based, with advance payment milestones that create negative working capital.

Investor implication: JSW concentration is the key buyer-power risk — loss of a single JSW order round could meaningfully dent intake. However, the qualification moat means JSW cannot easily switch mid-project. Advance-payment structure is a significant protection.

Force 4

Bargaining Power of Suppliers

🟡 Moderate Threat

Key inputs include specialized steel plates, electrical components, high-precision rollers, and furnace components — several of which are sourced from European and Japanese suppliers. The JCMI acquisition brings JCIL's German subsidiary (John Cockerill UVK) in-house, reducing one critical supplier link. Hedavali handles heavy fabrication, reducing third-party dependence for structural components. India's "China+1" manufacturing shift may ease sourcing of some components domestically over time.

Investor implication: Moderate supplier risk, improving. JCMI consolidation is explicitly a supply chain play as much as a revenue play — bringing European engineering capability in-house reduces the margin leakage to external suppliers.

Force 5

Threat of Substitutes

🟢 Low Threat (near-term)

Steel customers cannot cost-effectively in-source the engineering and manufacturing of a CGL or CGAL — these are billion-rupee capex projects requiring 15–18 months of specialist execution. The more interesting substitution risk is technological: could a competitor develop a disruptive alternative to conventional galvanizing lines? JCIL's JVD technology IS the disruptive alternative — making it both the defender and the disruptor. Volteron threatens blast furnace economics in the 2030s, a segment JCIL is positioning to lead. The substitute threat is a tailwind, not a headwind, for JCIL's technology portfolio.

Investor implication: This is a 10+ year story for the core business. JVD and Volteron extend the runway further. No near-term disruption risk to the core processing line business.

Overall Moat: Narrow-to-Wide — JCIL's moat on conventional processing lines is narrow (global competitors exist, pricing is contested). But its technology portfolio (JVD exclusive + Volteron partnership) is the foundation of a widening moat in specialty steel. No Indian-listed peer competes in this technology segment. The closest analogues globally — SMS Group, Danieli — are not publicly listed in India.
Narrow moat today, widening via JVD and Volteron — the technology bet is where the upside lives.

Chapter Four

The Vertical Stories

Vertical 1 · Largest Segment

Galvanizing, Coating & Pickling Lines

● Running

Continuous galvanizing lines (CGL), combined annealing and galvanizing lines (CGAL), colour coating lines, and acid pickling facilities. This is JCIL's established core — the AMNS Hazira CGAL (800+ metres, largest in company history), Tata Steel Kalinganagar CGL, and JSW Steel CGL#3 (₹300 Cr, due May 2028) all sit here. External catalyst: India's automotive sheet and construction sheet demand growing at 8.5% CAGR through 2030. Government's PLI scheme subsidises production of coated products. The India Cold Rolled and Coated Steel market is projected at USD 20 billion by 2033. This vertical provides the visible, bankable revenue base.

Vertical 2 · Fastest Growing

CRNO / Electrical Steel Processing Lines

▲ Ramping

Cold-rolled non-oriented (CRNO) steel is the critical material for EV motor cores and industrial motors. Cold-rolled grain-oriented (CRGO) is used in power transformers. India currently imports over 80% of its CRNO/CRGO requirement. The JSW-JFE Steel ₹5,845 Cr investment at Nashik/Vijayanagar is the anchor catalyst. JCIL's ₹1,300 Cr CRNO contract from JSW Vijayanagar Metallics (June 2, 2026) — covering two annealing and coating lines and one annealing and pickling line — is the largest single project in this vertical. PLI Scheme has lowered investment thresholds for CRGO to ₹3,000 Cr and 50,000 TPA. This vertical is the highest-margin, highest-growth segment of the decade.

Vertical 3 · Highest Margin

Value Services — Revamps, Spares & Roll Coating

● Running & Growing

Every processing line JCIL installs creates a 20–25 year aftermarket opportunity. Spares and revamps now represent 20–25% of the CY2025 order book — up materially from prior years. In January 2026, JCIL inaugurated India's first HP-HVAF (High Pressure High Velocity Air Fuel) roll coating facility at Taloja. The technology coats furnace rolls used in high-temperature processing lines, extending their operational life. Capacity: 300 rolls per year. Revenue: EUR 3 million (₹27 Cr) per year. Capex: EUR 2 million — a 1.5× revenue payback in the first year. The installed base of 30+ commissioned lines across India creates a captive aftermarket that grows as the project order book delivers.

Vertical 4 · The Option Value

JVD & Volteron — Green Steel Technologies

◆ Pre-Commercial / First Orders Imminent

Two disruptive technologies co-developed with ArcelorMittal sit at the frontier of JCIL's portfolio. JVD (Jet Vapour Deposition): A new galvanizing technique that is twice as fast as conventional methods, requires no zinc bath, and produces a thinner, more uniform coating. Over 1.1 million tonnes already coated at ArcelorMittal's Belgium facility. JCIL holds exclusive Indian commercialisation rights. Management guided the first commercial JVD order in India for Q1 CY26. Volteron™: A cold electrolysis process that extracts iron from ore using electricity — no blast furnace, no coal, zero CO₂. The world's first industrial-scale Volteron plant (40,000–80,000 TPA) is targeted to start in 2027. Management believes Volteron will enter a first commercial contract in 12–24 months. A Volteron contract would be transformational in scale — ironmaking equipment represents ~80% of a steel mill's total capex.

Four verticals: core galvanizing (revenue now), CRNO electrical steel (revenue ramping), services (margin engine), and JVD/Volteron (option value worth watching closely).

Chapter Five

The Strategic Insight

The JCMI acquisition is being reported as an inorganic revenue bolt-on. It is not. It is the Belgian parent, John Cockerill SA, systematically transferring its entire global metals technology headquarters to its India-listed subsidiary. The January 1, 2026 completion brought JCIL the German (John Cockerill UVK), Belgian, and Chinese metals businesses. The US metals transfer follows as Phase II. When complete, a Belgian engineering conglomerate will have its metals division — including JVD and Volteron technology rights — headquartered in a company listed on the NSE in Mumbai. That is arguably unprecedented for a company of this technology sophistication.

Why does this matter for investors? Because the promoter is not treating JCIL as an outsourced manufacturing arm or a regional contractor. The management explicitly stated their ambition: "You are investing in a global technology company out of India — this kind of company is very rare on the market, not more than four or five in total." The 5-year interest-free payment terms for the JCMI acquisition, the transfer of technology IP to India, and the stated ₹8,000 Cr revenue aspiration by 2030 all point in the same direction. The Belgian parent wants JCIL to become the world's leading listed technology company for the steel value chain — not just a project contractor in one market. The India listing is a feature, not a constraint.

JCMI isn't a revenue deal — it's a Belgian parent re-domiciling its global metals technology engine to the NSE.

Chapter Seven

Risks — Eyes Open

⚠️

Extreme Valuation — 200× PE Requires Perfect Execution

At ~₹3,940 Cr market cap against CY2025 standalone PAT of ₹10 Cr, the stock trades at approximately 394× trailing standalone earnings. The valuation is only defensible if consolidated earnings (post-JCMI) reach ₹60–80 Cr in CY2026 and scale significantly in CY2027. Any integration delay, project slippage, or margin disappointment on JCMI will trigger a multiple compression that standalone revenue growth cannot offset. The stock has gained 98% in 12 months; a correction to half the current price would still leave it richly valued.

Signal to watch: Q2 CY26 consolidated results — if PAT does not show a meaningful step-up from Q1's ₹7.36 Cr, re-rating risk is real.

⚠️

JSW Customer Concentration — One Client, Multiple Projects

The most recent ₹1,300 Cr CRNO order (JSW Vijayanagar), the ₹300 Cr CGL#3 (JSW Steel Coated Products), and a prior ₹1,750 Cr JSW order collectively suggest JSW entities account for over half the current order book. While each JSW entity is a distinct legal entity, a strategic decision by JSW to pause or slow steel capex — for example, due to global steel demand softness or a financing constraint — would materially compress JCIL's intake for 2–3 quarters.

Signal to watch: JSW Steel quarterly capex guidance and their capacity utilisation announcements.

⚠️

226-Day Debtors — Working Capital Can Turn Ugly

Debtor days of 226 as of December 2025 is the highest in a decade for JCIL. In project-based business, large project completions often involve milestone disputes or client acceptance delays, leaving large receivables outstanding. The cash conversion cycle has been volatile historically (from +79 days to –179 days). A single large project where the customer delays SAT (Site Acceptance Testing) approval can spike working capital requirements and force the company to draw on credit lines. JCMI's integration adds European and Chinese receivable dynamics that management has not yet disclosed.

Signal to watch: Quarterly debtors figure — any increase above 250 days on the consolidated balance sheet is a red flag.

⚠️

JCMI Integration Risk — Three Geographies, One Quarter of Experience

The JCMI acquisition closed January 1, 2026. Q1 CY26 is literally the first quarter of consolidated operations. JCML includes businesses in Belgium, Germany (John Cockerill UVK), and China — each with its own culture, contracts, working capital profile, and management team. The consideration structure includes a debt convertible into shares after 5 years if unpaid — adding a potential future dilution overhang of ~EUR 24.6M (~₹220 Cr). The US metals transfer is still pending (Phase II), adding another integration milestone. Until CY2026 full-year consolidated results are in, integration quality is guesswork.

Signal to watch: JCMI's revenue and EBITDA disclosure in quarterly results — specifically whether European margins are additive or dilutive.

⚠️

JVD / Volteron Commercial Delay — Option Value Is Not a Revenue Line Yet

The market is partially pricing in JVD's commercialisation. Management guided the first commercial JVD order for Q1 CY26 — there has been no public announcement of this order yet. Volteron's first industrial plant is targeted for 2027, but is at CRL7 (development stage) — with 12–24 months to a first contract. Green technology adoption in India's steel sector is a regulatory and economic push, not a pull; if global carbon pricing frameworks stall, the timeline for Volteron could extend materially. At the current market cap, a delay of 2 years in JVD/Volteron would still leave the standalone business fairly valued — but would remove the upside catalyst that justifies the premium.

Signal to watch: Any BSE announcement of a JVD commercial order. Silence beyond Q2 CY26 is a yellow flag.

Five specific risks: extreme valuation, JSW concentration, 226-day debtors, JCMI integration opacity, JVD commercial delay.

Verdict

Where Are We in the Story?

Story Stage

Early-to-Mid Chapters — The Revenue Ramp Has Arrived; The Technology Re-Rating Has Not

John Cockerill India is carried by three structural tailwinds that are real and durable: India's steel specialty capex cycle (PLI ₹43,800 Cr, CRGO ramp, EV supply chain), the company's unique position as the only India-listed high-technology steel equipment supplier, and the JCMI consolidation that transforms it from an ₹358 Cr regional contractor into a potential ₹1,250+ Cr globally-headquartered technology company. The quality signal in the financials is modest but improving — ROCE recovering to 12.2%, OPM expanding from negative to 11.7% at peak, cash from operations turning positive (₹159 Cr in CY2025 vs –₹66 Cr in CY2024). The near-term catalyst is already in play: the JSW CRNO ₹1,300 Cr order (June 2026) and the Q1 CY26 consolidated revenue ₹344 Cr suggest the execution step-up has begun, and Q2 CY26 results will be the first true test of the consolidated entity. On valuation, the stock at ₹7,879 (PE 200–220× trailing standalone, PB 18.5×) is aggressively priced for a narrative that is only partially proven — this is a stock where being directionally right on the 3-year story but wrong on the entry price can still cost you significantly. We are early-to-middle chapters: the revenue ramp has arrived, the technology re-rating (JVD, Volteron) has not.
