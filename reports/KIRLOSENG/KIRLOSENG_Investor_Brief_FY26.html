The Big Picture
Context — Why This Company, Why Now
India is in the middle of a structural power infrastructure supercycle. Grid reliability remains inconsistent for industrial users, data centre construction is exploding, and the government's nuclear power ambitions are accelerating. Kirloskar Oil Engines sits at the intersection of all three demand streams—and it is the only Indian company cleared to supply emergency gensets to nuclear power plants.

The India diesel genset market was valued at USD 1.33 billion in 2026 and is projected to reach USD 1.88 billion by 2031 at a 7.2% CAGR (IMARC Group). Within this, the high-horsepower segment (375 kVA and above, the tier dominated by KOEL's Optiprime and HHP products) is growing at a faster 8.75% CAGR, driven specifically by hyperscale data centres and large construction projects requiring synchronised, emission-compliant sets.

Three structural forces are converging simultaneously. First, India's data centre capacity is expected to triple by 2028 according to JLL India, with investments of over USD 50 billion announced. Every hyperscale facility needs N+1 standby power — and at 5–10 MW per facility, that translates directly into KOEL's sweet spot of 2,500–3,000 kVA Optiprime systems. Second, the Indian government's PM KUSUM scheme (₹34,422 Cr allocated) and broader rural electrification push sustain KOEL's traditional LHP genset and pump-set demand. Third, the Nuclear Power Corporation of India (NPCIL) has 21 reactors under various stages of construction as part of India's target to triple nuclear capacity to 22,000 MW by 2032 — each reactor requires emergency diesel gensets that meet stringent international safety standards.

The combined tailwind from data centre power backup, nuclear plant emergency power, and India's maritime power indigenisation creates a multi-year demand runway that transcends the traditional cyclical genset story. KOEL is not riding one wave; it is riding three at once, all at different stages of their respective build-out cycles.

Data centres, nuclear power, and navy indigenisation create a structural demand floor that makes KOEL's growth less cyclical than the diesel genset label implies.

The Engine Room
Business Model — What KOEL Actually Does
Three revenue engines run inside one listed entity: a genset manufacturing business (B2B), a pump-set and farm equipment business (B2C), and a systemically important NBFC called Arka Fincap. Understanding this structure is essential — it explains the apparent paradox of strong earnings growth alongside consistently negative free cash flow.

B2B segment is the core and the growth engine. KOEL manufactures diesel and gas engines across a power range of 2.1 kVA to 3,000 kVA (with custom solutions up to 6.3 MW). The product ladder runs from LHP (low-horsepower, below 500 kVA), which serves residential backup, telecom towers, and small industrial facilities, up to HHP (high-horsepower, 1,000 kVA+), which is where data centres, hospitals, nuclear plants, and large infrastructure clients buy. The flagship HHP product is the Optiprime Dual Core—a paralleled twin-engine architecture that can deliver up to 3,000 kVA from a compact footprint, and up to 5,200 kVA in paralleled configurations. KOEL is also the only Indian company that holds NPCIL qualification for emergency safety gensets—a certification that took years of testing and is essentially non-replicable by any domestic competitor in the near term.

The customer stickiness mechanism in B2B is layered. Data centre clients run KOEL gensets on multi-year AMC (Annual Maintenance Contract) arrangements; NPCIL has a multi-decade supply relationship; Indian Navy projects involve 7–10 year qualification and delivery cycles. The aftermarket and distribution business — spare parts, oil, batteries, service touchpoints — provides a high-margin annuity revenue stream that grows proportionally with the installed base. Every engine KOEL sells today is a customer for 10–15 years of service revenue, making the installed base the real long-duration asset of this business.

B2C segment covers pump sets (diesel, petrol, kerosene-based) for agriculture and rural water supply, plus electric pumps and farm equipment. This is a lower-margin, high-volume business that benefits from government rural electrification schemes but lacks the pricing power of B2B. Revenue from B2C has been growing steadily but is not the primary growth driver.

Arka Fincap (wholly owned NBFC, RBI-classified Systemically Important) provides corporate, MSME, and real estate financing. AUM stood at ₹7,256 Cr as of March 2025, with retail AUM exceeding 64% of book, GNPA of 1.2%, and NNPA of 0.3%. CRISIL has assigned AA (stable) to long-term borrowings and A1+ to short-term. Arka is funded through market borrowings, which flow through the consolidated balance sheet as debt — explaining the ₹5,819 Cr in borrowings that would look alarming without context.

KOEL's installed base is an annuity; every diesel engine sold today creates 10+ years of service revenue, making market share gains structurally compounding.

Competitive Structure
Porter's Five Forces

KOEL operates in a market where scale, certification moats, and relationship tenure matter far more than price. The structural picture is generally favourable, but two forces deserve active monitoring.

Force Rating Specific Rationale
Rivalry Intensity Medium Cummins India dominates the organised genset market (approx. 35–40% share by value) and commands a premium PE re-rating (~47x). KOEL is the #2 organised player but is rapidly closing in the HHP segment where Cummins's Indian manufacturing footprint is smaller. Greaves Cotton, Mahindra Powerol, and SDMO compete at LHP tiers; MTU (Rolls-Royce) and Caterpillar compete at very high HHP. Pricing competition is moderate — buyers in the data centre and nuclear segment select on certification and reliability, not price.
Buyer Power Medium Data centre hyperscalers (Adani, Hiranandani, CtrlS) and large infra clients can negotiate volume discounts, but NPCIL and Indian Navy are sovereign customers with limited alternatives — they MUST use KOEL or import (no other Indian company is NPCIL-qualified). Top 5 customers' share of revenue is not disclosed, but the distribution of 50,000+ annual genset units across diverse end markets implies no single buyer has excessive leverage in the LHP tier.
Supplier Power Medium Key inputs are copper (for alternators) and pig iron/steel (for engine castings). Both commodities saw price spikes in FY25–26, and management acknowledged partial rather than full pass-through. Imported components — fuel injection systems from Bosch, and electronic controls — remain a single-source dependency risk. KOEL manufactures engines in-house but sources certain sub-assemblies, limiting full commodity insulation.
Threat of New Entrants Low Greenfield entry requires ₹500–1,000 Cr minimum capex, 5–7 year relationship-building for key accounts, and multi-year testing cycles for NPCIL qualification (which KOEL holds and no new Indian entrant has passed). CPCB-IV+ emission compliance standards introduced in 2023 further raised the bar, as they require on-board diagnostics and sophisticated emission controls that newer or unorganised players cannot easily implement.
Threat of Substitutes Low-Med Natural gas gensets are a growing alternative for industrial users with piped gas access, but penetration in India is limited to ~5% of the installed base. UPS and battery storage are adequate for telecom towers and small offices but cannot replace diesel at the MW scale required by data centres. Hydrogen gensets are emerging (KOEL itself unveiled India's first prototype), but commercial deployment is 5+ years away. The primary competitive threat from substitutes is in the 10–200 kVA residential/small-commercial tier, not KOEL's focus HHP segment.
Synthesis: The two highest-pressure forces are Supplier Power (copper and pig iron volatility directly compress margins when pass-through is incomplete) and Rivalry Intensity in HHP (Cummins will not cede market share without a fight as data centre demand grows). Both are manageable but require active monitoring each quarter.
KOEL's NPCIL certification is a structural moat with no near-term replicability — it is the most defensible part of the entire competitive position.

Revenue Engines
The Vertical Stories — Five Growth Vectors
PowerGen • High Horsepower
Data Centre Backbone: The HHP Surge

The PowerGen segment grew 32% year-on-year in FY26, making it KOEL's fastest-growing division. Within this, the HHP sub-segment (gensets above 1,000 kVA) is the primary engine. KOEL's market share in HHP is approaching double digits — up from a near-negligible position three years ago — because the company invested early in the Optiprime Dual Core architecture at a time when data centre developers were scrambling for large, certified standby power systems.

The landmark order that signals KOEL's arrival in this tier was the HyperNext 192 MW contract — 96 units of 2,500 kVA Optiprime systems, described as one of the largest single deployments of high-capacity standby power in India's history. KOEL now has HHP products up to 3,000 kVA commercially deployed and can configure up to 6.3 MW through parallelisation. Management expects data centre orders to constitute a double-digit share of PowerGen revenue by FY28.

Status: Ramping hard. 50,000+ genset units sold in FY26 (+41% in diesel generator sales). New 50,000-engine capacity (₹700 Cr capex) comes online April 2027; additional 20,000 HHP engines (₹1,400 Cr) follow within 2 years.

Industrial • Defence • Nuclear
Critical Infrastructure: Nuclear, Navy, and Nation-Building

The Industrial segment grew 22% in FY26. Within this, two strategic programmes define KOEL's long-term defensibility. For NPCIL, KOEL is the sole Indian supplier of emergency diesel generator sets for nuclear reactor safety systems. With 21 reactors under construction as India targets 22,000 MW nuclear capacity by 2032, KOEL has decades of sovereign order flow in this niche. The NPCIL programme involves engines that undergo 5+ years of testing and qualification — no competitor has cleared this hurdle domestically.

For the Indian Navy, KOEL is developing high-power marine diesel engines as part of the government's Defence indigenisation push. This involves multi-year R&D programmes funded partly through DRDO partnerships. Management referenced a strategic Navy project in the Q4 FY26 concall without disclosing specifics, consistent with defence confidentiality requirements. KOEL also unveiled India's first hydrogen-powered genset prototype in 2026, positioning itself as a future supplier to hydrogen-ready naval and industrial applications.

Status: Strategic, long-gestation, high-margin. NPCIL deliveries are ongoing and will accelerate with reactor construction. Navy and hydrogen are 3–5 year payoff stories.

International Business
Going Global: Middle East, Americas, and the Pivot from Exports

KOEL's international business crossed ₹1,000 Cr in gross sales in FY26, growing 37% year-on-year. Management is explicitly transitioning from a traditional export model (ship from India, sell through distributors) to a proper international business model (local hiring, local presence, local after-sales infrastructure). The Middle East — where 40–50% of international revenue currently originates — is the primary market. The Americas are the targeted growth frontier. Africa and Southeast Asia are secondary markets.

The strategic logic is sound: KOEL gensets are price-competitive with international peers, and India's CPCB-IV+ emission standards align with European/GCC regulations, making Indian-manufactured units exportable to compliance-heavy markets without modification. The risk, acknowledged by management, is that Middle East political instability could disrupt 40–50% of export revenue in a tail scenario.

Status: Running well, building local infrastructure. Growth expected to sustain at 25–30% annually through FY28 if Middle East remains stable.

B2C • Agriculture • Pumps
The Rural Annuity: Pump Sets and Farm Equipment

KOEL's B2C business manufactures engine-based and electric pump sets for agriculture, aquaculture, and domestic water supply. This business is slower-growth (single-digit to low-double-digit revenue growth) but important as a distribution channel into rural India. It benefits from government schemes including PM KUSUM (₹34,422 Cr for solar pump deployment, which displaces some diesel pumps but also expands KOEL's addressable market for solar-diesel hybrid solutions). The aftermarket and service component of this business is high-margin and recurring.

Status: Steady, reliable, not the growth story. Distribution & aftermarket grew 15% in FY26. Contributes stable margins to the consolidated P&L.

Financial Services • NBFC
Arka Fincap: The NBFC Inside the Genset Company

Arka Fincap is a wholly owned, RBI-registered Systemically Important NBFC offering corporate, MSME, and real estate loans. AUM: ₹7,256 Cr (March 2025), with 64%+ retail book. Asset quality is clean — GNPA 1.2%, NNPA 0.3%, CRISIL AA stable. Arka was originally conceived to provide working capital solutions to KOEL's dealer and customer network, but has evolved into a standalone institutional lender. It raises capital through market borrowings (bonds, CPs, bank lines), which is why KOEL's consolidated debt of ₹5,819 Cr looks alarming on first read — the vast majority of this is Arka's lending capital, not manufacturing leverage.

The NBFC embedded inside a manufacturing company is a structural complexity that equity investors frequently misprice in both directions. A strong Arka adds earnings and ROE diversification; a stressed Arka could require capital from the parent at the worst possible moment — during a manufacturing capex cycle. The current 1.2% GNPA is not a concern, but the trend in this metric over the next 4–6 quarters is the most important Arka-specific watch signal.

Status: Growing well, asset quality healthy, rate cycle risk exists. Rising interest costs (₹483 Cr in FY25 vs ₹9 Cr in FY16) reflect Arka's scale-up and are the primary drag on consolidated net margins.

Five growth vectors at different stages: HHP/data centre (ramping), nuclear/navy (strategic moat), international (building), B2C/pumps (steady), Arka Fincap (financial wildcard).

The One Thing
Strategic Insight — What Most Investors Miss
The Non-Obvious Truth

KOEL is not a diesel genset manufacturer that also happens to have an NBFC. It is a capital-allocation machine that uses genset cash flows to fund an NBFC lending book, which in turn grows assets and interest income, which funds more genset R&D. The free cash flow is structurally negative by design — not by distress — because Arka Fincap consumes capital as it grows its loan book. The moment investors separate the manufacturing P&L from the NBFC balance sheet, the picture clarifies entirely: the manufacturing business is healthy, profitable, and margin-expanding; the NBFC is a separate financial business being scaled with borrowed capital.

The practical implication is counterintuitive: the right way to value KOEL is as a sum-of-parts — manufacturing business (PE multiple on standalone earnings) plus NBFC franchise (price-to-book on Arka's equity) — not as a single conglomerate PE. When investors treat the consolidated negative free cash flow as a manufacturing red flag, they are confusing NBFC capital deployment with operating deterioration. When the NBFC's AUM growth slows (or it raises external equity), the consolidated cash flows will look dramatically better, even if the manufacturing business hasn't changed at all.

There is a second layer to this insight: KOEL's capex in HHP is also a customer acquisition tool for Arka Fincap. Data centre developers who buy KOEL's Optiprime systems on credit arrangements, or MSME industrial buyers who finance a 750 kVA genset, often become Arka Fincap borrowers. The business model creates a vertically integrated loop — sell the engine, finance the engine, service the engine — that is more valuable than any of the three legs in isolation.

The third non-obvious element: KOEL's NPCIL qualification is a regulatory moat, not a product moat. The specific gensets KOEL supplies to nuclear plants are not dramatically different in engineering from its regular industrial sets — but the qualification process (5+ years, multiple independent safety audits, site integration testing) means any competitor must spend the same time regardless of their technical capability. This is a barrier built on bureaucratic clock cycles, not patents — and those are often the most durable barriers of all.

Value KOEL as sum-of-parts: manufacturing standalone earnings (PE) plus Arka Fincap book value (P/B) — consolidated free cash flow is misleading as a standalone signal.

Revenue Trajectory

KOEL's consolidated revenue has grown from ₹3,296 Cr (FY21) to ₹7,701 Cr (FY26) — a 5-year CAGR of approximately 18.5%. The pace has accelerated: 3-year CAGR is 16%, and TTM (trailing twelve months through Dec 2025) is already at ₹7,334 Cr, implying full-year FY26 consolidated growth exceeds 21%. Margin expansion from 9% OPM (FY18) to 19% OPM (TTM) is the quality signal — KOEL has used operating leverage to convert revenue growth into faster profit growth (3-year PAT CAGR of 39% versus 16% revenue CAGR).

Valuation Context

At ₹1,613 per share (Apr 2026) and market cap of ₹23,451 Cr (rising to ~₹27,930 Cr by mid-June 2026 as the stock continued its run), KOEL trades at approximately 43x trailing PE. For comparison, Cummins India trades at ~47x on lower revenue growth but higher ROCE. The market is pricing KOEL at a slight discount to Cummins, which appears reasonable given that KOEL's ROCE of 13.7% is below Cummins's ~30%+ (Cummins India is a leaner, non-NBFC business). However, the discount does not adequately credit KOEL's faster revenue growth trajectory (25% standalone vs Cummins's ~12–15%), its nuclear/defence moats, and the optionality embedded in HHP capacity coming online in FY27. The stock has returned 117% over the last year and 61% annually over 3 years, suggesting the market is partially re-rating the story — but the full HHP thesis is not yet priced in.

What the market is pricing in: A normalization of standalone EBITDA margins at 13–14%, HHP volume growth of 20–25% annually, and Arka Fincap maintaining its current credit quality. The market is NOT yet pricing in the possibility that KOEL breaks above the 15% EBITDA margin threshold (which becomes achievable if HHP mix exceeds 35% of PowerGen revenue), nor is it pricing a significant re-rating of Arka Fincap as a standalone financial franchise.

Eyes Open
Risks — Specific, Concrete, Measurable
⚠
Arka Fincap Credit Cycle — The Hidden Risk
Arka's GNPA of 1.2% looks clean today, but the NBFC is in a rapid AUM expansion phase (₹7,256 Cr AUM, retail-heavy). History shows MSME and real estate lending NBFCs can deteriorate quickly in a credit tightening cycle. KOEL's consolidated interest costs have risen from ₹9 Cr (FY16) to ₹483 Cr (FY25) — all Arka-driven. If GNPA creeps above 3%, KOEL may need to inject equity into Arka, diverting capital from manufacturing capex. Signal to watch: GNPA trend in Arka's quarterly numbers, and whether provisioning coverage falls below 70%.
⚠
HHP Capex Execution vs. Demand Materialisation
The ₹1,400 Cr HHP capacity investment assumes data centre demand remains robust through FY28 when the capacity comes online. If hyperscale spending in India decelerates (a global trend in H1 2026 with some US tech companies pausing datacentre commitments), KOEL could commission capacity into a softer market. Asset turns of 4–5x are management guidance, not contractual commitments. If actual turns are 2–3x, the investment return is mediocre. The HyperNext 192 MW order is a proof point, but a single order does not validate a ₹1,400 Cr capex thesis. Signal: order book disclosures for HHP specifically — if management begins providing this metric, it's a positive; if not, opacity itself is a flag.
⚠
Raw Material Inflation — Copper and Pig Iron
Copper is a key input for alternators; pig iron and steel for engine castings. Both have seen volatile price movements in FY25–26. Management has acknowledged only partial pass-through of input cost increases to customers. If copper prices spike another 15–20% (plausible in a sustained China demand recovery scenario), KOEL's EBITDA margins could compress 80–120 bps — reversing the FY26 expansion that was a key re-rating trigger. Signal: gross margin trend in quarterly results; any management commentary about pass-through lag duration.
⚠
Middle East Geopolitical Disruption
40–50% of KOEL's international revenue originates from the Middle East. A serious escalation in regional conflict (Gulf states, Israel-Iran) could disrupt project site access, delay payment cycles, and reduce new infrastructure tenders. KOEL has been building local presence in the region to partially mitigate this, but revenue concentration remains high. FX volatility (USD/AED movements) is a secondary concern. Signal: international revenue quarterly run rate — any deceleration from the ~₹250 Cr/quarter pace should be interrogated for geo cause.
⚠
Promoter Dilution Narrative
Promoter holding has declined from 59.3% (FY17) to 41.1% (Dec 2025) — a 18 percentage point reduction over 8 years. While some of this reflects secondary market sales and the FY22–23 dilution that likely funded Arka Fincap's expansion, the trend bears watching. At 41%, promoter commitment remains adequate, but further dilution could signal capital stress or change governance dynamics. The Screener "Cons" column already flags this. Signal: any regulatory or SEBI filings showing promoter pledge creation or further stake reduction.
Arka Fincap's credit quality is the most underrated risk in KOEL's investment thesis — it can impair the manufacturing capex story if it deteriorates at an inconvenient moment.

4. Standalone EBITDA Margin Trajectory

5. Capex Progress vs. Timeline — Kagal April 2027 Milestone
What to track: management updates on the ₹700 Cr LHP/LMH capacity (target: April 2027 online) and ₹1,400 Cr HHP complex (2-year horizon). Direction: on-time commissioning. Five minutes of quarterly research: HHP mix commentary, Arka GNPA, international revenue, standalone EBITDA margin, Kagal capex progress—these five cover 90% of the investment thesis.
Investment Verdict
The Powermen Behind India's Buildout

Kirloskar Oil Engines sits at the intersection of three of India's most capital-intensive structural buildouts: data centres requiring multi-megawatt standby power, nuclear power plants requiring certified emergency gensets, and naval indigenisation requiring marine diesel engines. The PowerGen segment's 32% growth and the international business crossing ₹1,000 Cr in FY26 confirm that the structural demand thesis is converting into revenue — not just management aspiration. KOEL's EBITDA margin expansion from 12.2% to 13.1% in a year when raw material costs were volatile demonstrates genuine operating leverage, and the installed base of 50,000+ annual genset sales creates a compounding service revenue annuity that makes the business increasingly resilient.

The financial quality signal is nuanced: ROCE of 13.7% is adequate but not exceptional, constrained by the capital-intensive NBFC inside the consolidated entity. Stripping Arka Fincap out, standalone manufacturing ROCE would be materially higher. Free cash flow is structurally negative by design, not distress — a distinction that matters enormously for valuation. The ₹2,100 Cr capex cycle through FY28, if executed on time and absorbed at guided asset turns, will add ₹7,500–9,500 Cr of peak revenue capacity — more than bridging the gap to KOEL's USD 2 billion FY30 target. The near-term re-rating catalyst is April 2027: when the first Kagal capacity block comes online and management reports its first full quarter of contribution, the market will have hard evidence for the capex-to-revenue bridge.

At 43x trailing PE against 25% standalone revenue growth and 35% PAT growth, the valuation is not cheap — but it is not wildly expensive relative to Cummins India's 47x on slower growth. The premium is warranted only if HHP momentum is sustained and Arka Fincap's credit quality holds. Both conditions are currently met. This is a mid-chapter story: execution has been credible, structural tailwinds are intact, and the next 18 months will determine whether this is a long-term compounder or a cyclical with a great story.

Why not A (Highly Credible): Three active watch flags prevent upgrade to Tier A. First, the USD 2 billion FY30 target is aspirational and has not been validated by the capex milestones being reached. Active flags: (1) Kagal April 2027 milestone — first hard test; (2) Arka Fincap GNPA trend — currently clean but must stay clean; (3) HHP order book — needs disclosure before Q1 FY28 to validate capex rationale. Review tier after Q2 FY27 results.
