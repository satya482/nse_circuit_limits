01
Context · Why now
The 300-million-tonne furnace — a demand boom that everyone saw coming

Every tonne of steel, cement, glass and aluminium made in India passes through a furnace lined with refractory material — heat-resistant bricks and mixes that survive temperatures above 1,200°C and get consumed in the process. India is the world's second-largest crude steel producer, and its National Steel Policy targets 300 million tonnes of steel capacity by FY2030-31 (from ~165 MT today) and 400 MT by FY2035-36. Refractory demand is mechanically chained to that expansion.

The near-term numbers support the story. India produced roughly 169 MT of crude steel in FY26 (up ~11%) and is projected to reach ~183 MT in FY27 (+8%), with capacity rising ~6% to ~248 MT. Steel consumption has doubled in a decade — from 77 MT in FY15 to 152 MT in FY25. The India refractories market is worth roughly USD 2.6–2.8 billion, with growth estimates ranging from ~3% CAGR (conservative, IMARC) to ~8.3% CAGR through 2030 (Grand View Research), and iron & steel taking ~65% of demand. Management's own blended estimate on the Q4 call: end-industry volume growth of about 6% — steel ~8%, non-ferrous ~4%, cement in between.

So why is the stock down ~22% over one year? Because the supply side saw the same forecast. Vesuvius commissioned 250,000 tonnes of new capacity at Visakhapatnam in late 2024; Calderys is ramping the largest greenfield refractory plant ever built in India (Odisha, five production lines, production from late 2025); Monolithisch is expanding; and Chinese import competition persists — while RHIM itself added ~300,000 tonnes via the Dalmia acquisition in 2023. The result is an industry where demand grows 6% a year and capacity grows faster, producing what management bluntly calls "aggressive pricing behaviour" and competitors trying to "fill up their plant at any cost." The entire listed sector has de-rated in sympathy: RHIM −22%, Vesuvius India −21.6%, IFGL −32% over one year.

The moment is interesting precisely because of this tension. The structural demand story is intact and government-anchored — infrastructure capex, urbanisation, supply-chain localisation. But FY26–FY28 is the digestion phase: the window where new capacity floods in ahead of the demand that justifies it, and where the companies with the strongest cost position, stickiest customer relationships and cleanest balance sheets take share from those who merely own kilns. That is the contest this brief evaluates RHIM against.

Chapter 1 takeawayIndia's refractory demand grows ~6% a year toward the 300 MT steel target, but announced rival capacity grows faster through FY27 — volume tailwind, price headwind.
02
Business model · The flywheel
Selling uptime, not bricks

Strip away the metallurgy and RHIM's business is simple: it sells consumables that are a rounding error in the customer's cost sheet but catastrophic if they fail. Refractory linings are typically around 2–3% of a steelmaker's production cost, yet a lining failure stops the entire furnace — so customers buy reliability first and price second, and switching suppliers means re-qualifying products inside a running plant. That asymmetry is the root of the moat.

RHIM is the Indian subsidiary (56.07% held) of RHI Magnesita N.V., the world's largest refractory group, which mines its own magnesite and dolomite and runs 35+ production sites globally. The Indian arm — formerly Orient Refractories — operates nine sites including Bhiwadi (Rajasthan), Visakhapatnam (Andhra Pradesh), Cuttack (Odisha) and the plants acquired from Dalmia Bharat Refractories, serving 700+ customers with roughly a 30% share of the Indian market. The product set spans the full furnace: slide gate plates, tundish nozzles, purging systems, magnesia-carbon bricks, castables and monolithics.

The flywheel

Revenue compounds through consumption, not projects. Every tonne of steel poured erodes lining; every cement kiln campaign ends in a reline. The flywheel: install base → recurring consumption tied to customer tonnage → total refractory management (TRM) contracts where RHIM staff run the customer's entire refractory operation → deeper process data → higher-value solutions (robotics, digital monitoring, recycling) → harder to displace. The 4PRO platform is the formalisation of this last stage: performance-based long-term agreements bundling products with automation and CO₂-reduction services. Three 4PRO contracts were signed in January 2026 alone, and RHIM deployed India's first fully integrated robotic solution in caster operations during FY26, with two robots now running.

What they can do that others cannot

  • Parent-group access to captive magnesite mining and global R&D — critical, since magnesia raw materials are globally scarce and USD-priced; mines are now being transferred into RHIM's own name at roughly half the market purchase cost.
  • Only player with full-line capability across shaped + unshaped + flow control + services at Indian scale — most rivals are strong in one niche (IFGL in flow control, Calderys in monolithics).
  • Balance-sheet depth to sign multi-year performance contracts and absorb startup costs that smaller rivals cannot carry.
Chapter 2 takeawayA recurring-consumable business with qualification lock-in: 30% India share, 700+ customers, and a service layer (TRM/4PRO) that converts brick sales into multi-year contracts.

03
Vertical stories · One card per engine
Four engines, running at different speeds
Vertical 1 · Steel & ironmaking · ~3/4 of the franchise · Status: RUNNING + RAMPING

The core: ladles, casters, and now coke ovens

Steel is the anchor: FY26 growth came from ladle solutions, electric arc furnace projects, slide-gate flow control and the ironmaking push (MIP, COCO1 and PRA projects). The external catalyst is the National Steel Policy's 300 MT capacity target and ~8% steel output growth. The newest engine is a 30,000+ tonne coke-oven refractory order — two batteries for one of India's largest integrated steel producers — giving an 18-month secured order book, continuous kiln utilisation and fixed-cost absorption. Management counts five more coke-oven batteries coming up in 2–3 years and conservatively expects to win at least two. Crucially, coke-oven bricks are fed by RHIM's newly-transferred captive mines, so this is share gain with a raw-material cost advantage attached.

Vertical 2 · Cement & lime · 11% of revenue (down from 13%) · Status: UNDER SIEGE

The battlefield vertical

Cement demand itself is fine — infrastructure spend, housing recovery, GST rationalisation. The problem is supply: rival capacity built over the last 18 months is now in production and being dumped into cement at marginal prices. RHIM's cement revenue share fell from 13% to 11% in FY26 as it walked away from loss-making volume ("we choose to do only the business that makes sense for us"). The counterplay is converting large cement customers from transactional brick supply to solution partnerships — early response described as very good, but unproven in the P&L. Seasonality note: cement's strong season is May–September; October–January is lean, which dented Q4 volumes.

Vertical 3 · Industrial non-cement (glass, non-ferrous, energy, petrochem) · Status: RESTARTING

The project-cycle vertical, plus a new door into petrochemicals

FY25/FY26 was described as the worst period for non-cement industrial project orders; that book is now rebuilding, with several conversions in hand. The structural addition is the Resco acquisition, which opens the petrochemical refractories segment for FY27 — a new end-industry with India's refinery and cracker capex as the external driver. Also in this bucket: the 51:49 recycling JV with Khemka (announced 25 June 2026) for a greenfield refractory-recycling facility in Odisha, expected Q3 2026 — circular-economy raw material at the doorstep of India's steel belt.

Vertical 4 · 4PRO solutions & automation · Status: RAMPING (margin story, not revenue line)

The de-commoditisation engine

4PRO bundles refractories with robotics, digital lining monitoring, scanning, process optimisation, recycling and CO₂ reduction into long-term performance agreements. Three contracts signed in January 2026; India's first fully-integrated caster robot deployed; two robots operational with more planned. The near-term P&L effect is actually negative — startup costs (people, machines) hit other expenses before revenue arrives — but each signed contract converts commodity volume into annuity-like, spec-locked revenue. This vertical is small in rupees and central to the thesis.

Chapter 3 takeawaySteel/ironmaking carries FY27 (coke-oven order book + captive mines); cement is a managed retreat; petrochem and 4PRO are the optionality.
04
Strategic insight · The one thing
The impairment is the strategy, not the accident
⚡ The non-obvious insight

Read the ₹655 crore goodwill write-off not as a stumble but as a strategy memo. In 2023 RHIM paid up to buy capacity — the Dalmia plants — on the thesis that owning Indian tonnage ahead of the 300 MT steel boom was the moat. Three impairments in four years (~₹1,600 Cr cumulative) are management's own accounting admission that in a market where everyone can build tonnage, tonnage is not a moat. What the company is building instead is a two-sided replacement: on the cost side, captive mines transferred into its own name at roughly half the market price of magnesite — which flips raw-material inflation from a threat into a weapon, because input inflation raises industry prices while RHIM's own cost stays mined-in-house (the CEO's telling remark: "raw material price increase is good for the industry"). On the revenue side, 4PRO performance contracts that price uptime instead of bricks. The company the market is valuing is a brick maker in a price war; the company management is building is a mining-backed industrial-services annuity. The gap between those two descriptions closing — or not — is the whole investment case.

The corollary is a discipline test. A tonnage company defends every order; a solutions company walks away from bad business — which RHIM visibly did in cement (share of revenue 13% → 11%). If future quarters show volume defended at collapsing margins, the old thesis is secretly back. If margin recovers on flat-to-modest volume outgrowth, the new thesis is working.

Chapter 4 takeawayWatch whether RHIM behaves like a brick seller (defend volume, bleed margin) or a solutions-and-mining franchise (walk from bad business, rebuild margin) — the impairment says management chose the latter.

What the market is pricing in

At ~43x adjusted FY26 earnings — versus Vesuvius India at ~35x (mcap ₹9,295 Cr) and IFGL at ~30–33x (mcap ₹1,300 Cr) — the market is paying a leadership premium while the whole sector de-rates. Risk register · Eyes open
Five specific ways this goes wrong
1 · The price war outlasts the guidance
Calderys' Odisha plant — the largest greenfield in Indian refractory history — only began production in late 2025 and will spend FY27 filling lines "at any cost," exactly when RHIM needs 1–3% price increases to stick. If realised pricing slips, the 13% margin guide fails for a fourth consecutive time.
Signal: gross margin trend Q1–Q2 FY27; any new mention of "pricing pressure" offsetting announced hikes.
2 · A fourth impairment
~₹1,600 Cr has been written off in three tranches (FY23 ~₹649 Cr, FY24 ~₹309 Cr, FY26 ~₹655 Cr) against the 2023 acquisitions. Management now states "no further restructuring is required… with utmost confidence." Residual goodwill/investment value remains on the books (standalone investments ₹1,392 Cr); another export-demand or FX deterioration could force a fourth cut and destroy that specific promise.
Signal: export volume commentary; INR/USD beyond ~₹90; any auditor emphasis-of-matter in H1 FY27.
3 · Magnesia cost squeeze with a lag
Fused magnesia and DBM costs rose on Chinese energy prices and freight (Middle East disruption), hitting Feb–Mar 2026 shipments; high-cost inventory is still flowing through the P&L. Raw materials are USD-denominated against a depreciating rupee. The mines transfer mitigates this only for magnesite-based lines — alumina and imported grades remain exposed.
Signal: raw-material-to-revenue ratio; inventory days (138 in FY26) failing to normalise; freight commentary.
4 · Coke-oven concentration and the 18-month cliff
The fixed-cost-absorption story for FY27 leans heavily on one 30,000+ tonne coke-oven order with one large customer. Post the 18-month book, continuity requires winning ≥2 of the 5 future batteries — a management expectation, not a contract. A loss to Chinese or domestic bidders re-opens the fixed-cost hole in ironmaking.
Signal: new battery award announcements FY27–FY28; ironmaking segment commentary each call.
5 · Steel-cycle deceleration under the target
The 300 MT policy target is not a contract either: FY27 capacity growth is already moderating (~6%) as steelmakers turn cautious on margins and geopolitics, and RBI has trimmed GDP growth to 6.9%. Refractory demand follows actual furnace-hours, not policy documents — a steel capex pause flows straight into RHIM volumes with a one-quarter lag.
Signal: India crude steel monthly run-rate vs the ~183 MT FY27 projection; customer capex deferral news.
Chapter 6 takeawayThe top three risks — price war, fourth impairment, input costs — all compress the same line: the 13% margin guide has no slack for any of them.

The next two quarters are the referendum — this is a "prove-it" holding, not a discovery story.
